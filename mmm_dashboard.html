💡 Plain-language summary

Think of each channel as a vending machine. You put dollars in, and new customers come out. Google Shopping gives you 50 new customers per $1,000 inserted. 6-model comparison — 52-week holdout test R²

Models trained on 200 weeks, tested on the last 52 weeks they never saw. Negative R² means worse than predicting the average every week.

💡 Why these results prove Ridge was the right choice

All four linear models land within 1% of each other (0.44–0.45 Test R²) — this convergence means the data's signal is fundamentally linear after our transformations. XGBoost's Train R² of 1.00 means it memorized the training data perfectly, then failed completely on new data (Test R² = −1.93). Random Forest shows the same pattern. With only ~200 training weeks, tree models don't have enough data to learn generalizable patterns — they just learn noise. The right tool for this problem is Ridge Regression.

How adstock decay was tuned per channel
Decay rates shown.
💡 What is adstock decay?

When you see a Google Search ad, you buy almost immediately — the effect is short-lived (decay ≈ 0.2). When you see a YouTube brand video, the impression might influence your purchase 3 weeks later — the effect lingers (decay ≈ 0.8). Decay rates were automatically tuned per channel using cross-validation, not assumed.

The saturation problem — why Meta Facebook is stuck
Saturation curves shown.
💡 What is saturation?

Every ad channel has a ceiling. The first $100k on Google Shopping generates huge lift. At $5M, each extra dollar barely moves the needle. Meta Facebook is spending at a level where the curve has completely flattened — the company is on the plateau. Google Shopping still has runway before it saturates.

⚠️ One important caveat before cutting Meta

Meta may drive brand awareness that converts later through Google Search — a pattern called "view-through attribution" that this model cannot fully capture. Before cutting Meta Facebook budget, the recommendation is a geo holdout test: turn off Facebook in one region for 4 weeks and measure whether Google Search conversions drop in that region. If they don't, the Meta cut is safe to execute.

Methodology

How the model works.

Four steps turn raw ad spend data into reliable business decisions — each one solving a real problem that naive analysis gets wrong.

Ad

Adstock Transform

Ads don't stop working when spend stops. Adstock models the "echo" — an impression from last week still influences today's purchase. Decay rates tuned per channel via cross-validation.

Sat

Hill Saturation

Every channel plateaus. The Hill function maps spend to a 0–1 intensity score that reflects real diminishing returns — preventing the model from suggesting "double spend = double customers."

Rdg

Ridge Regression

Google channels run simultaneously (multicollinearity). Ridge regularization prevents unstable, overfit coefficients — giving stable, interpretable attributions even with correlated inputs.

CI

Bootstrap Uncertainty

500 bootstrap resamples propagate uncertainty through to the final ROI numbers. Every recommendation comes with a 95% confidence interval — so you know which findings are solid and which need testing.

💡 Why not just use Meta's own data?

Platform-reported attribution (Meta's "Ads Manager") counts conversions that would have happened anyway. If someone searches for your brand on Google and then clicks a Meta ad, Meta claims the conversion. MMM solves this by looking at aggregate patterns over time — correlating weekly spend changes with weekly purchase changes across all channels simultaneously, without relying on any platform's self-reported numbers.

💡 Why seasonality controls matter

November and December sales are 2× the baseline — not because of ads, but because of Black Friday and holiday shopping. If we don't add calendar controls (Q4 dummy, Black Friday dummy), the model wrongly attributes the holiday spike to whichever channel was spending most in November — usually Meta. The controls ensure media effects are isolated from seasonal demand.
